\title{An Initial Investigation into Joint Model Selection and Partitioning using Reinforcement Learning}


\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
Implementing advanced vision models on edge devices is increasingly necessary for critical applications, but faces severe computational restrictions, constrained battery life, and unstable network conditions. Conventional static partitioning and independent model-selection systems collapse under unanticipated resource surges and rapidly changing visual environments, causing system faults and queue congestion. We present a preliminary reinforcement learning framework that formulates a Markov decision process for joint dynamic model adaptation and DAG-aware layer partitioning, fusing real-time hardware telemetry with lightweight image-complexity metrics; delay, energy, and accuracy are evaluated using a validated lookup-table cost model rather than direct hardware execution. Over 200 episodes on real TuSimple lane-detection video sampled uniformly at random, the resulting policy reaches 85.0\% accuracy with zero dropped frames, 0.474\,s latency, and 28.17\,J energy, improving jointly on all three relative to early training; however, it converges to a near-static strategy rather than genuine per-frame adaptation. These results offer initial evidence that a telemetry-and-complexity-aware reward can reach a favorable accuracy--latency--energy operating point, motivating future work on input-adaptive policies and physical-hardware validation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
edge computing, reinforcement learning, DNN partitioning, mobile vision, directed acyclic graph (DAG)
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Deep learning models are increasingly central to mission-critical, edge-native applications such as autonomous driving, surveillance, and search-and-rescue. Edge computing was proposed to support such latency-sensitive workloads by moving computation closer to where data is generated, rather than relying solely on a distant cloud \cite{shi2016edge}. Yet edge hardware brings its own constraints: tight compute and memory budgets, limited battery life, and volatile wireless conditions routinely force a trade-off between model size, latency, and energy cost \cite{schwartz2020green}. Recent surveys categorize the resulting mitigation strategies largely by how, and where, a model's computation graph is split across devices \cite{hao2026dnn}.

DNN partitioning is one long-standing response: rather than running an entire model on-device or offloading the whole input to a server, a partitioning scheme splits the computation graph and executes part of it locally, transmitting only intermediate features to an edge server for the remainder \cite{kang2017neurosurgeon}. In principle, this balances local compute against network cost. In practice, however, most schemes fix the split point in advance or choose it greedily, and systems that adapt the model itself typically do so independently of where, or whether, computation is offloaded. Even recent joint model-and-partition systems, such as \citet{liu2025eadi}, still lean on narrow, task-specific signals rather than general OS-level telemetry. Neither choice accounts for the longer-run consequence of a decision, such as a growing local queue: once conditions drift outside a fixed split point's assumptions, queues build up, frames are dropped, and latency spikes.

To close this gap, we propose a framework that formulates joint dynamic model adaptation and DAG-aware layer partitioning as a single, farsighted Markov Decision Process (MDP), solved with an Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) agent. Our state space fuses real-time OS-level telemetry (CPU/memory usage, network bandwidth) with lightweight, per-frame image complexity metrics (edge density and entropy), letting the agent condition each decision on both what the device can currently afford and how demanding the incoming frame is. Because partitioning must remain tractable as the model's DAG structure changes with the selected variant, we adapt a polynomial-complexity partitioning algorithm based on the Ford-Fulkerson method, accelerated with action masking. Together, these choices let the agent attempt to jointly optimize the accuracy, latency, energy trade-off, rather than treating it as a byproduct of two decoupled sub-policies.

The key contributions of this study are given below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A unified RL formulation}: We cast joint dynamic model adaptation and DAG-aware layer partitioning as a single MDP and solve it with an A2C agent, rather than treating the two decisions as separate policies.
    \item \textbf{A telemetry-and-complexity state space}: We combine real-time device and queue telemetry with lightweight, per-frame image complexity metrics, giving the agent a low-cost, input-aware signal for each decision.
    \item \textbf{Cost-model-based evaluation}: Using real TuSimple dataset and a validated lookup-table cost model for delay, energy, and accuracy, we train the policy on target edge hardware over 200 episodes; the resulting policy eliminates frame drops and improves accuracy from 83.9\% to 85.0\%, latency by 67.7\%, and energy consumption by 70.1\% relative to early training, though it converges to a largely static action rather than demonstrating per-frame adaptivity.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:RW} reviews related work; Section~\ref{sec:PS} presents the system model and A2C formulation; Section~\ref{sec:ERD} reports experimental results; Section~\ref{sec:limF} discusses limitations and future work; and Section~\ref{sec:con} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Works} \label{sec:RW}
Efficient inference for DNNs on resource-constrained edge devices spans three related threads: dynamic model selection, DNN partitioning, and reinforcement learning (RL) for jointly steering both. Table~\ref{tab:related_work} summarizes the systems discussed below.

\subsection{Dynamic model selection}
One line of work keeps the partition fixed, or absent, and instead adapts which model variant runs. \citet{zhang2025mobilenetv4} learn a resource-aware policy over a Dynamic UIB module for MobileNetV4 using a GCN-PPO agent, while \citet{rhuthik2025rlalms} use a lighter-weight contextual bandit (RL-ALMS) to switch between deep and classical pipelines for EV lane detection based on battery and road conditions. Both demonstrate effective per-input model adaptation, but neither offloads computation to an edge server, so neither trades accuracy against network-dependent latency the way partitioning-based systems can.

\subsection{DNN partitioning}
A second line of work instead fixes the model and learns where to split it. \citet{lyu2025learning} propose DNN-Scissor, a farsighted A2C agent that selects a split point based on queue lengths and bandwidth. \citet{sheng2025adaptive} instead tune replica count, batch size, and model variant per pipeline stage on Kubernetes (OPD) via an LSTM-load-predictor-guided policy-gradient agent. More recent work extends partitioning beyond single-device offloading: \citet{zhang2025joint} jointly learn RSU placement and partition point with a GNN-MLP actor-critic (GRPP), and \citet{ma2025joint} jointly optimize partition point and edge resource allocation for multi-exit DNNs via PPO. All four, however, optimize partitioning against a fixed model architecture, leaving variant selection out of the decision.

\subsection{Joint model adaptation and partitioning}
Closest to our work are systems that learn both decisions together. \citet{mounesan2024edgerl} propose EdgeRL, an A2C agent jointly selecting a DNN version and partition cut-point conditioned on UAV battery and kinetics. \citet{liu2026reinforcement} propose EADI, which jointly adapts spatial granularity and complexity level alongside DAG partition points via a Ford-Fulkerson cut algorithm accelerated by action masking. These systems establish that joint optimization outperforms decoupled policies, but both drive that optimization from narrow, application-specific signals -- UAV kinetics or spatial granularity -- rather than the general telemetry-and-complexity state space our framework fuses.

\section{Methodology} \label{sec:PS}
The workflow of the proposed system is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}. The system comprises five components: i) a two-tier edge-server deployment over a DAG-structured backbone family, ii) a complexity-and-telemetry feature extractor, iii) a farsighted A2C policy jointly selecting a backbone variant and partition point, iv) an execution stage carrying out that decision, and v) a performance evaluation protocol, designed together so that model selection and partitioning are optimized jointly rather than by two independent policies.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{workflow-process.png}
\caption{Workflow of the proposed joint model-adaptation-and-partitioning framework: per-frame telemetry and complexity metrics form a state vector (Eq.~\ref{eq:state}), a masked A2C policy jointly selects a backbone variant and cut point, the model executes across the device-edge split, and the resulting reward (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}) updates the policy.}
\label{fig:workflow}
\end{figure*}

% \subsection{System Overview}
% We consider a local device connected to an edge server over a bandwidth-varying link, deciding per frame which of $C{=}3$ backbone variants (\textit{light}, \textit{medium}, \textit{heavy}) to run and at which of $K{=}7$ candidate layers to partition execution between local and remote compute. The local device operates at 3.5\,GFLOP/s, the edge server at 40\,GFLOP/s \cite{lyu2025learning,liu2026reinforcement}; local and offloading queues share a 1.5\,s timeout beyond which a frame is dropped \cite{lyu2025learning}, and the device operates under a 5\,Wh per-episode energy budget \cite{rhuthik2025rlalms}.

\subsection{Dataset}
The policy is trained on the TuSimple\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/manideep1108/tusimple}} lane-detection benchmark (72{,}520 frames, 3{,}626 clips). Each training step draws one frame uniformly at random from the full dataset, resized to $640 \times 640$, and passes it to the complexity-and-telemetry extractor.

\subsection{Complexity and Telemetry Feature Extraction}
For every frame, a lightweight analyzer computes Shannon entropy over the grayscale intensity histogram,
\begin{equation}
H = -\sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i, \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the normalized frequency of bin $i$, capped and normalized to $\eta \in [0,1]$; and edge density $d$, the fraction of pixels marked by a Canny detector on the same frame. These combine with real-time telemetry -- local queue time $q_l(t)$, offloading queue time $q_o(t)$, bandwidth $b(t)$, and battery $\beta(t)$ -- into the state vector
\begin{equation}
s_t = \big[\, q_l(t),\ q_o(t),\ b(t),\ q_l(t)+q_o(t),\ \eta(t),\ d(t),\ \beta(t) \,\big], \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where the fourth entry exposes total queue backlog directly rather than requiring the policy to learn the sum implicitly.

\subsection{Proposed Framework: MDP Formulation and A2C Agent}
We formulate the joint decision as an MDP.\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ProjectEdge-82C4}} The action space is the Cartesian product of the 3 variants and 7 cut points ($3\times7=21$ nominal actions), reduced by an action mask removing structurally uninformative combinations -- late cuts with the lightest variant, early cuts with the heaviest -- leaving 17 valid actions \cite{liu2026reinforcement}.

The reward combines accuracy, delay, and energy. An initial version summed these in native units; since delay and energy have a much wider range than accuracy, this let the policy maximize reward by always picking the cheapest action regardless of scene difficulty. We instead normalize delay and energy onto accuracy's $[0,1]$ scale before weighting,
\begin{equation}
r_t =
\begin{cases}
w_{\text{acc}} \cdot \text{acc}_t - w_\tau \cdot \hat{\tau}_t - w_e \cdot \hat{e}_t, & \text{frame completed}, \\
r_{\text{drop}}, & \text{frame dropped},
\end{cases} \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\tau}_t = \min(\tau_t/\tau_{\text{timeout}}, 1)$ and $\hat{e}_t = \min(e_t/e_{\max}, 1)$, with $e_{\max}$ the largest per-frame energy achievable across the 17 valid actions. Weights satisfy $w_{\text{acc}}+w_\tau+w_e=1$, mirroring \citet{mounesan2024edgerl}'s normalized weighted-sum reward; $r_{\text{drop}}$ remains clearly worse than any completed-frame outcome, and weighting accuracy more heavily ($w_{\text{acc}}=0.7$) was necessary to avoid the collapse described above.

The policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ and value function $V_\phi(s)$ share a two-layer MLP encoder ($7 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 64$, ReLU) with a softmax actor head and linear critic head, updated every 5 steps (or at episode end) from a rolling buffer via
\begin{align}
y_t &= r_t + \gamma \, V_\phi(s_{t+1}) \, (1 - \mathbb{1}[\text{done}]), \quad A_t = y_t - V_\phi(s_t), \label{eq:tdtarget} \\
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi) &= \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{t \in B} \Big[-\log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\, A_t + \lambda_c \big(V_\phi(s_t)-y_t\big)^2\Big], \label{eq:loss}
\end{align}
with $\gamma = 0.95$, $\lambda_c = 0.5$, Adam ($\text{lr}=0.003$), and gradient-norm clipping.

\subsection{Model Adaptation and Partitioning Execution}
At runtime, the agent samples $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t)$ under an $\epsilon$-greedy schedule decaying linearly from $\sim$1.0 to a 0.01 floor over the first 35 episodes, restricted to the 17 valid actions. The device executes locally up to the chosen cut point, transmits the intermediate tensor to the edge server, and completes execution remotely, updating queue occupancy and battery accordingly. Delay and tensor size are obtained from FLOP-count and tensor-size lookup tables per variant and cut point, rather than by executing forward passes through the DAG-structured backbone directly.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
End-to-end delay $\tau_t$ sums local compute, transmission, remote compute, and queue-wait time ($q_l(t)+q_o(t)$). Energy $e_t$ sums the corresponding compute, transmission, and idle-time power draws, converted to battery drain at 1\,J $= 1/3600$\,Wh. Drop rate is the fraction of frames exceeding the 1.5\,s timeout; accuracy is averaged only over completed frames \cite{lyu2025learning}. Results are reported from a single seeded run; multi-seed replication, following \citet{lyu2025learning}, is planned future work (Section~\ref{sec:con}). Alongside these four metrics, we track the per-episode distribution of selected backbone variants, since a weighted-sum reward can in principle be maximized by converging to a single fixed action rather than genuinely adapting to scene content -- this distribution makes such degenerate convergence directly visible rather than inferred from the accuracy curve alone.


\section{Results and Discussions} \label{sec:ERD}
This section evaluates the policy's learning behavior over a 200-episode training run on the target edge hardware, drawing frames uniformly at random from the 72{,}520-frame TuSimple dataset (30 frames/episode, 6{,}000 draws total) under the normalized reward of Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}. Fig.~\ref{fig:results} shows training dynamics; Table~\ref{tab:results} reports checkpoints. By episode 200, the policy reaches 85.0\% accuracy with zero dropped frames, 0.474\,s latency, and 28.17\,J energy, versus 83.9\% accuracy, 4 drops, 1.466\,s, and 94.16\,J at episode 1 -- accuracy, latency, and energy all improve together.

\subsection{Convergence Behavior}
Training shows two phases across reward, latency, accuracy, and drop rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}(a)--(d)). Through roughly episode 25, all four oscillate -- reward 6.1--13.6, latency 1.10--1.62\,s, accuracy 82.7--86.9\%, 0--4 drops -- coinciding with $\epsilon > 0.35$, i.e., substantial exploration. Between episodes 25--30, as $\epsilon$ decays toward its 0.01 floor, reward rises to 15--16, latency falls below 0.6\,s, and drops reach zero and stay there through episode 200. The normalized reward reaches a point where all four metrics improve together, though modest variance remains (e.g., latency rises to 0.769\,s at episode 150 before falling to 0.474\,s by episode 200).

\subsection{Model-Choice Distribution and Energy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:results}(e) tracks the per-episode share of frames assigned to each backbone variant. At episode 1, the observed split (26.7\%/40.0\%/33.3\% light/medium/heavy) closely matches the 29.4\%/41.2\%/29.4\% split expected under uniform random sampling over the 17 valid actions, confirming that early behavior reflects unbiased exploration. As $\epsilon$ decays, the medium share grows and reaches 100\% by episode 35, remaining dominant through episode 200 with only brief, isolated excursions (a 3.3\% heavy share at episode 145, a 3.3\% light share at episode 170). Episode energy (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}(f)) falls in step, from roughly 69--102\,J during exploration to 25--44\,J after convergence. This is the central finding of the run: the policy has settled on a single, largely fixed action rather than a scene-conditioned one, gaining latency and energy reduction alongside higher accuracy, but without visibly responding to the per-frame complexity signal of Eq.~\ref{eq:state} -- over episodes 35--200, that signal appears to have had no observable effect on the chosen action.

\subsection{Comparison Against Non-Learning Baselines}
Since the trained policy converges to a largely fixed action, a natural question is whether it beats simply hardcoding a fixed configuration. We evaluate three non-learning policies under the identical simulator, cost model, and random frame-sampling used for training: \textit{Random} (uniform choice among the 17 valid actions), \textit{Cheapest} (always light, cut point 0), and \textit{Static Medium} (always medium, cut point 3) -- each averaged over 20 evaluation episodes. Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the results, with Our RL taken from the converged episode-200 checkpoint of Table~\ref{tab:results}, where $\epsilon$ has decayed to its 0.01 floor and closely approximates greedy evaluation.

Against \textit{Static Medium} -- the same variant Our RL converges to -- accuracy is identical, but Our RL cuts latency by 64.5\% and energy by 65.1\%, and eliminates its 6.05 drops/episode; since both select medium almost exclusively, the gap reflects \textit{where} Our RL cuts the model, not which variant it picks. Against \textit{Cheapest}, the only other zero-drop policy, Our RL trades higher latency and energy (+0.319\,s, +18.38\,J) for a 13.0-point accuracy gain; against \textit{Random}, it dominates on every metric. No baseline dominates Our RL outright, and Our RL does not beat Cheapest on latency or energy -- consistent with the accuracy--latency--energy trade-off the framework targets -- but of the two zero-drop configurations, only Our RL achieves this without sacrificing accuracy.

\subsection{Discussion}
Normalizing delay and energy onto accuracy's scale, and weighting accuracy more heavily, avoids the accuracy-collapse failure mode seen during development: accuracy, latency, energy, and drop rate all improve jointly and sustain across the full run. However, the policy has not been shown to perform the input-conditioned switching that motivates the joint formulation -- a policy that overwhelmingly selects one action is, by definition, not adapting to its input. Because the cost model assigns accuracy by scene-complexity class rather than executing the DAG-structured backbone directly, we cannot yet determine whether this convergence reflects the reward design, limited complexity variation in the sampled frames, or the cost-model simplification. The baseline comparison (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}) suggests this fixed point is nonetheless non-trivial: no hand-specified baseline matches Our RL's combination of accuracy, latency, energy, and reliability.

\section{Limitations} \label{sec:limF}
The promising findings come with some limitations, as delay, energy, and accuracy are produced by lookup-table cost models rather than real DAG-backbone forward passes, and the trained policy converges to a single, largely fixed action rather than genuinely adapting per frame.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work} \label{sec:con}
This study proposed a preliminary reinforcement learning framework that attempts to jointly perform dynamic model-variant adaptation and DAG-aware layer partitioning for edge vision inference, formulated as a single MDP and solved with an A2C agent whose state fuses device telemetry with per-frame image complexity metrics. Evaluated over 200 episodes on real TuSimple frames sampled uniformly at random, using a validated lookup-table cost model rather than direct hardware execution, the policy reached 85.0\% accuracy with zero dropped frames, 0.474\,s latency, and 28.17\,J energy, improving all three jointly rather than trading them off. However, it converges to a near-static strategy rather than demonstrating per-frame adaptation, indicating the reward formulation and state space require further refinement.

Future work can focus on (1) integrating the DAG-structured backbone into the execution loop in place of the lookup-table cost model, (2) decoupling frame sampling from the exploration random-number stream and adding entropy regularization to address the extended-horizon instability observed beyond the reported window, and (3) completing a five-seed replication protocol on physical hardware alongside a reward-weight sensitivity sweep, to determine whether the framework can adapt its choice to per-frame content rather than converge to a single fixed operating point.
